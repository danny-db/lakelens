### AI & Model Serving

AI calls (ai_query, model endpoints) can spend faster than the daily bill updates. This category watches them by the hour so a runaway gets caught the same day, not on next month's invoice.

#### H0 Runaway AI spend

- **What it finds:** Watches the account's AI token rate hour by hour, so a runaway shows up the same day, before the daily bill even updates.
- **How to read it:** The account's busiest AI hour this week (every endpoint and caller together), set against its usual busiest hour: the median of its busiest hour in each of the prior four weeks. vs_normal is how many times that normal this week's peak is, and that sets the alert. Each row is one (endpoint, caller) pair inside that peak hour with its share of the hour's tokens, so the top row is who drove the spike. “est_usd_rough” is an estimate built from your own recent bill; read it as a relative signal (“who spiked”), not an exact invoice.
- **What to do:** Layer three controls: (1) a SQL alert on the hourly rate so a spike is caught fast; (2) a daily billing-dollar alert as the reconciling truth; (3) a monthly budget as a coarse net. Prevent repeats by not granting expensive models (Sonnet/Opus-class) to every pipeline by default - default to a cheaper tier (e.g. Haiku), cap max_tokens, and only run the model over NEW rows each refresh, never the whole table again.
- **Alerts:** Account tokens in its busiest hour this week (a peak) versus the account's normal (median of the account's busiest hour in each of the prior four weeks): high at 3x, medium at 2x, medium when the account has no prior history; rows are the top contributors with their share
- **Prove the fix with:** M2 Exact $ per job and per pipeline
- **Reads:** `system.ai_gateway.usage`, `system.billing.list_prices`, `system.billing.usage`

### Idle Compute

Compute you are paying for while it does little or no work - warehouses that never switch off, clusters running at a fraction of their capacity, or background tools that keep the lights on around the clock.

#### H3 Chatter keeps warehouses awake

- **What it finds:** Checks how many do-nothing statements (ones that read no data) the account fired this week against its usual week; each one resets a warehouse's auto-stop timer, so the warehouse never sleeps. Then names the tools firing them.
- **How to read it:** The number of statements across the account that read no data this week, set against its usual week (the median of the prior four weeks). vs_normal is how many times that normal this week is, and that sets the alert. Each row is one client with its share of that chatter. “client_application” and “executed_by” name the tool exactly: this is configuration, not guesswork. “stmts_per_session near 1” means the tool opens a fresh connection for every statement.
- **What to do:** Fix the tool first - e.g. a BI tool's refresh schedule left at its aggressive default, or a monitoring tool that should have its own small warehouse. Then move always-on tools onto a small serverless warehouse where a short auto-stop is practical.
- **Alerts:** Account zero-read statements this week (a total) versus the account's normal (median of the account's prior four weekly totals): high at 3x, medium at 2x, medium when the account has no prior history; rows are the top contributors with their share
- **Prove the fix with:** M3 The “After” Scoreboard - weekly $ per warehouse
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.compute.warehouses`, `system.query.history`

#### H4 Warehouses that never sleep

- **What it finds:** Shows which warehouses have no ‘stopping’ events - a signal they may be billing continuously instead of auto-stopping when idle.
- **How to read it:** For each warehouse, look for the presence and recency of STOPPING / STOPPED events. No STOPPING over two weeks is a signal to investigate - NOT proof of waste. It might genuinely run 24/7 for real load. Confirm with its actual billed usage (M3) before acting.
- **What to do:** If a warehouse never stops AND a chatty client is hitting it (see the Chatter hunt), fix the client or move it to its own small serverless warehouse. If it never stops because of genuine round-the-clock load, that is fine - just confirm the size matches the load (see Queue Pressure).
- **Alerts:** Informational (no alert)
- **Prove the fix with:** M3 The “After” Scoreboard - weekly $ per warehouse
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.compute.warehouse_events`, `system.compute.warehouses`

#### H14 Idle cores

- **What it finds:** Shows the busiest clusters and their average CPU - sustained low CPU on big machines means you are paying for cores that sit idle.
- **How to read it:** This lists the busiest clusters over the last 7 days with their average CPU. Sustained CPU well below your other busy clusters on large nodes means idle cores you are paying for. Pick a cluster to drill into (in the notebook, query its hour-by-hour CPU and memory); low steady CPU with low memory = downsize, memory near full = memory-bound.
- **What to do:** Right-size on this evidence: start with a small cluster and step up only when the metrics say so. Use fewer, larger nodes for shuffle-heavy work rather than many small ones.
- **Alerts:** Informational (no alert)
- **Prove the fix with:** M2 Exact $ per job and per pipeline
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.compute.clusters`, `system.compute.node_timeline`

#### H16 Jobs running on All-Purpose compute

- **What it finds:** Checks how many automated job runs the account put on All-Purpose (interactive) clusters this week against its usual week. All-Purpose bills a higher rate per DBU than Jobs compute for the same work, so these runs pay a premium.
- **How to read it:** All-Purpose compute is for interactive notebook work and bills a higher list rate than Jobs compute. A job pinned to an always-on All-Purpose cluster pays that premium AND the idle time between runs. The account's job runs on All-Purpose clusters (cluster_source UI/API) this week are set against its usual week (the median of the prior four weeks). vs_normal is how many times that normal this week is, and that sets the alert. Each row is one job with its share of those runs and the clusters it used. all_purpose_usd_dbu and jobs_usd_dbu are your own account's average list $/DBU for classic All-Purpose and Jobs compute over the last 30 days, and pct_saving is the rate saving those two imply.
- **What to do:** Move the job to Jobs Compute (an ephemeral cluster that spins up per run and terminates after) or Serverless Jobs. That drops it to the Jobs rate and removes idle-between-runs billing. Interim: shorten the cluster's auto-termination so it stops soon after the last run.
- **Alerts:** Account job runs on All-Purpose compute this week (a total) versus the account's normal (median of the account's prior four weekly totals): high at 3x, medium at 2x, medium when the account has no prior history; rows are the top contributors with their share
- **Prove the fix with:** M2 Exact $ per job and per pipeline
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.billing.list_prices`, `system.billing.usage`, `system.compute.clusters`, `system.lakeflow.job_run_timeline`, `system.lakeflow.job_task_run_timeline`, `system.lakeflow.jobs`

#### H18 Idle cores on interactive clusters

- **What it finds:** Checks how much of the account's interactive (All-Purpose) cluster capacity sat idle this week against its usual week, then names the clusters that idled most: big machines you pay for that mostly do nothing (the ds_highmem pattern).
- **How to read it:** Idle capacity is node-minutes weighted by how much of the CPU went unused: a node at a quarter of its CPU for an hour counts as three quarters of an hour idle. The account's idle node-minutes on interactive clusters this week are set against its usual week (the median of the prior four weeks). vs_normal is how many times that normal this week is, and that sets the alert: idle capacity growing far beyond the account's usual is the thing to catch. Each row is one interactive cluster with its idle node-minutes, its share of the account's, and its average and peak CPU. A cluster with a large share and a low average, especially a high-memory one, is paying for cores that do nothing.
- **What to do:** Right-size on this evidence: fewer / smaller nodes, or move the scheduled work to Jobs compute (which terminates per run). Shorten auto-termination so it can't sit idle for hours. For notebook work, serverless notebooks scale to zero.
- **Alerts:** Account idle node-minutes on interactive clusters this week (a total) versus the account's normal (median of the account's prior four weekly totals): high at 3x, medium at 2x, medium when the account has no prior history; rows are the top contributors with their share
- **Prove the fix with:** M2 Exact $ per job and per pipeline
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.compute.clusters`, `system.compute.node_timeline`

#### H19 Databricks Apps compute

- **What it finds:** Checks how much compute the account's Databricks Apps billed this week against its usual week. An app bills while it runs, whether or not anyone is using it.
- **How to read it:** A Databricks App bills DBUs while deployed and running, regardless of traffic. The DBUs every app in the account billed this week (complete days) are set against its usual week (the median of the prior four weeks). vs_normal is how many times that normal this week is, and that sets the alert, so apps that scaled up or started burning more stand out. Each row is one app with its DBUs, its share of the account's app DBUs and how many days of the week it billed; an app billed every day is effectively always-on. High DBU + always-on + little real traffic = a stop-or-consolidate candidate.
- **What to do:** Audit per-app traffic (Apps UI Insights, or OAuth-consent as a breadth signal); stop or undeploy apps no one uses, and consolidate per-workspace/per-env duplicates. Apps that only need business-hours availability don't need to run overnight.
- **Alerts:** Account Databricks Apps DBUs this week (a total) versus the account's normal (median of the account's prior four weekly totals (complete days)): high at 3x, medium at 2x, medium when the account has no prior history; rows are the top contributors with their share
- **Prove the fix with:** M2 Exact $ per job and per pipeline
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.billing.usage`

### Heavy Reads & Spill

Queries that read far more data than they need, or run out of memory and spill to disk. Both make jobs slower and more expensive than they should be.

#### H1 Read amplification

- **What it finds:** Checks how much data the account's MERGE statements read this week against its usual week, then names the MERGE targets behind it; an “incremental” job quietly re-scanning everything shows up here.
- **How to read it:** The TiB every MERGE in the account read this week, set against its usual week (the median of the prior four weeks). vs_normal is how many times that normal this week is, and that sets the alert. Each row is one MERGE target (or the job, notebook or person behind it) with its runs, the TiB it read this week and its share of the account's MERGE reads, so the top row is where the extra reading went. A high read-to-write ratio says the MERGE reads far more than it changes. If the target table is small but the reads are huge, the cost is upstream (the source query has no date filter). If each run reads about twice the target's size, the merge is scanning the whole target in both its match and rewrite phases.
- **What to do:** Bound the source with an incremental watermark so it only reads new rows; bound the target with incremental_predicates on a date column; cluster the target on that same column so old data can be skipped; and update only the rows that changed.
- **Alerts:** Account MERGE reads this week (a total) versus the account's normal (median of the account's prior four weekly MERGE read totals): high at 3x, medium at 2x, medium when the account has no prior history; rows are the top contributors with their share
- **Prove the fix with:** M4 Benchmark a fix - per-statement efficiency, week over week
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.billing.usage`, `system.lakeflow.job_run_timeline`, `system.lakeflow.jobs`, `system.query.history`

#### H2 Disk spill

- **What it finds:** Checks how much working data the account's queries spilled to disk this week (they ran out of memory, which is slower and you pay for the extra time) against its usual week, then names the sources behind it.
- **How to read it:** The TiB every statement in the account spilled to disk this week, set against its usual week (the median of the prior four weeks). vs_normal is how many times that normal this week is, and that sets the alert. Each row is one source of spilling statements (a job, notebook, saved query, dashboard or person) with its spill and share of the account's spill. Compare spill to the input size. Spill much LARGER than the input is a query-shape problem: a CTE recomputed many times, de-duplication done after a join instead of before, or an unbounded lookback. Spill roughly equal to the input is a sizing problem.
- **What to do:** Rewrite first - shorten lookbacks, stage a re-used CTE into a temp table, and de-duplicate the small side before joins. Only if a clean query still spills should you increase the warehouse/cluster size. Open the worst offender's query profile to see which step spills.
- **Alerts:** Account disk spill this week (a total) versus the account's normal (median of the account's prior four weekly spill totals): high at 3x, medium at 2x, medium when the account has no prior history; rows are the top contributors with their share
- **Prove the fix with:** M4 Benchmark a fix - per-statement efficiency, week over week
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.billing.usage`, `system.lakeflow.job_run_timeline`, `system.lakeflow.jobs`, `system.query.history`

#### H6 Unusually heavy scan hours across the account

- **What it finds:** Checks the account's heaviest scanning hour this week against its usual heaviest hour, then names who did the scanning; often one investigation re-scanning a raw table over and over.
- **How to read it:** The account's busiest scan hour this week (bytes read by everyone's SELECTs), set against its usual busiest hour: the median of its busiest hour in each of the prior four weeks. vs_normal is how many times that normal this week's peak is, and that sets the alert. Each row is one person who scanned in that hour, with their share of its bytes. One person with a large share and many scans (the scans column) is usually a single incident hunt: someone searching a raw event table with ‘LIKE %some-id%’ and re-paying the full scan on every refinement. Open the statements in Query History to confirm the shape.
- **What to do:** Give investigators a two-line template: first, time-bound the opening scan on the table's date column; second, stage that first pass into a temp table and run every refinement against the small staged copy, so the repeated scans become one. Longer term, promote the searched fields to real clustered columns, or evaluate a full-text search index.
- **Alerts:** Account's busiest scan hour this week (a peak) versus the account's normal (median of the account's busiest scan hour in each of the prior four weeks): high at 3x, medium at 2x, medium when the account has no prior history; rows are the top contributors with their share
- **Prove the fix with:** M1 Per-query attributed cost (shared warehouses)
- **Reads:** `system.query.history`

#### H21 Tables likely still on snappy (not zstd)

- **What it finds:** Finds Delta tables created before Databricks switched the default compression from snappy to zstd; zstd typically stores the same data in less space.
- **How to read it:** The compression codec isn't in any system table, so this can't read it directly. Instead it lists Delta tables created BEFORE the Oct-2025 default flip (snappy → zstd) per schema - these are the likely-snappy CANDIDATES. A table created after that, or one whose codec was set explicitly, may already be zstd. This is a 'worth checking' list, not proof.
- **What to do:** Confirm a candidate's codec with SHOW TBLPROPERTIES <tbl> ('delta.parquet.compression.codec') or by checking whether its files end in .snappy.parquet. To switch: set the table property delta.parquet.compression.codec = 'zstd', then rewrite the data so the new codec actually applies (OPTIMIZE, or for a full rewrite REORG TABLE) - setting the property alone only affects newly-written files. Prioritise the largest, most-scanned tables.
- **Alerts:** Schemas with likely-snappy Delta tables: medium whenever a row exists (a configuration state, no magnitude)
- **Prove the fix with:** M2 Exact $ per job and per pipeline
- **Reads:** `system.information_schema.tables`

### Long-Running & Repetitive

Statements that run for hours, or the same small query fired thousands of times. Individually easy to miss; together they add up to real money.

#### H5 Statements far longer than the account's usual

- **What it finds:** Checks the account's longest statement this week against the longest it usually runs in a week, and splits that time into waiting-for-capacity vs starting-up vs actually-running.
- **How to read it:** The account's longest statement this week, set against its usual longest: the median of the longest statement in each of the prior four weeks. vs_normal is how many times that normal this week's longest ran, and that sets the alert. Each row is one source (job, notebook, saved query, dashboard or person) with its longest statement this week; share_pct is that statement's length as a % of the account's longest, so the top row is the culprit. The split tells you which of three problems you have. Long execution time plus a huge read = a genuine heavy scan. Long execution time plus tiny reads = a HUNG statement (check execution_status and the query profile). A large queued time = a capacity problem (see Queue Pressure).
- **What to do:** Set a workspace-wide statement timeout so runaways stop themselves; the platform default is TWO DAYS. A few times the account's usual longest statement (the normal this check shows) is a sensible starting point, and long ETL can raise its own limit per session. Note: changing the workspace setting restarts running warehouses, so schedule it.
- **Alerts:** Account's longest statement this week (a peak) versus the account's normal (median of the account's longest statement in each of the prior four weeks): high at 3x, medium at 2x, medium when the account has no prior history; rows are the top contributors with their share
- **Prove the fix with:** M5 Timeout guardrail scoreboard
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.billing.usage`, `system.lakeflow.job_run_timeline`, `system.lakeflow.jobs`, `system.query.history`

#### H7 Repeated identical queries (polling)

- **What it finds:** Checks how often the account re-ran the exact same query this week, by the same identity within the same hour, against its usual week, then names the identities and statements that repeat most, typically a job, app or dashboard polling for changes.
- **How to read it:** A repeat is a run of a SELECT whose exact text and parameters the same identity already ran earlier in the same clock hour. The first run in each hour does not count, so a refresh scheduled hourly or less often never counts, while a loop that checks every few seconds or minutes counts every check after the first. The repeats across the account this week are set against its usual week (the median of the prior four weeks). vs_normal is how many times that normal this week is, and that sets the alert. Each row is one identity and statement with its share of the account's repeats. ‘hours_repeating’ is how many hours of the week it repeated in, ‘peak_runs_in_an_hour’ its busiest hour, and ‘from_cache_pct’ the share of runs the result cache answered, which use little compute. Open ‘example_statement_id’ in Query History to see the statement. For notebook and job code the recorded text can be the line of code that issued the query, so one row can cover a loop over several tables; the example shows which.
- **What to do:** Find what re-runs the statement (open ‘example_statement_id’ in Query History) and make it event-driven: a table-update trigger runs work only when the source changes. If a dashboard or app reads the same result again and again, point it at a cached or materialised result instead. Otherwise lower the frequency to what downstream actually needs.
- **Alerts:** Account repeat runs of identical SELECTs this week (a total) versus the account's normal (median of the account's prior four weekly totals of repeat runs): high at 3x, medium at 2x, medium when the account has no prior history; rows are the top contributors with their share
- **Prove the fix with:** M3 The “After” Scoreboard - weekly $ per warehouse
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.billing.usage`, `system.lakeflow.job_run_timeline`, `system.lakeflow.jobs`, `system.query.history`

### Right-Sizing & Config

Are your warehouses and clusters the right size and shape for the work? This category surfaces the settings - size, auto-stop, cluster count, queue pressure - so you can match capacity to demand.

#### H8 Queue pressure vs capacity

- **What it finds:** Checks how long the account's queries waited for capacity this week against its usual week, then shows which warehouses the waiting happened on, next to how long they actually ran: the objective signal for right-sizing.
- **How to read it:** The hours statements across the account spent queued for capacity this week, set against its usual week (the median of the prior four weeks). vs_normal is how many times that normal this week is, and that sets the alert. Each row is one warehouse that queued this week, with its share of the account's queue wait. High queued time relative to execution time means the warehouse is undersized or its maximum cluster count is too low for peaks. Near-zero queueing with low utilisation means there is room to size DOWN. This is real demand, not perceived slowness.
- **What to do:** Decide warehouse by warehouse, by load shape. Spiky, idle-heavy workloads suit serverless (scales in seconds, stops in minutes). Steady, predictable load can suit a right-sized classic warehouse with spot and committed-use discounts. Keep different workload types on separate warehouses so one noisy client can't queue everyone.
- **Alerts:** Account queue wait this week (a total) versus the account's normal (median of the account's prior four weekly queue-wait totals): high at 3x, medium at 2x, medium when the account has no prior history; rows are the top contributors with their share
- **Prove the fix with:** M3 The “After” Scoreboard - weekly $ per warehouse
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.compute.warehouses`, `system.query.history`

#### H9 Warehouse config audit

- **What it finds:** Lists the current settings of every warehouse - type, size, cluster range, auto-stop - with the last time each one changed.
- **How to read it:** One row per warehouse. Look for a long auto-stop on interactive workloads, ‘max_clusters = 1’ on a shared BI warehouse (no room to burst), and recent unexplained change times - the sign of config drift (a warehouse resized by hand with no audit trail).
- **What to do:** Agree a baseline for each warehouse (type, size, auto-stop) and re-run this audit weekly. Any difference from the baseline is a conversation, not a surprise.
- **Alerts:** Informational (no alert)
- **Prove the fix with:** M3 The “After” Scoreboard - weekly $ per warehouse
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.compute.warehouses`

#### H17 Warehouses with auto-stop disabled

- **What it finds:** Finds classic SQL warehouses set to never auto-stop (auto_stop_minutes = 0) - they bill 24/7 even when no queries are running.
- **How to read it:** A classic warehouse with auto_stop_minutes = 0 never suspends, so it bills around the clock regardless of use. min_clusters > 1 multiplies that. This is the cube_serving pattern - clusters held warm continuously. Each row is such a warehouse and its size.
- **What to do:** Set auto_stop_minutes to a short value that fits the workload's idle gaps so it suspends when idle, and drop min_clusters to 1 unless sustained concurrency needs more. For genuinely bursty BI, a serverless warehouse auto-stops in minutes.
- **Alerts:** Classic warehouses with auto-stop off: medium whenever a row exists (a configuration state, no magnitude)
- **Prove the fix with:** M3 The “After” Scoreboard - weekly $ per warehouse
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.compute.warehouses`

#### H22 Serverless jobs in Performance-optimized mode

- **What it finds:** Checks how much the account spent on serverless jobs in Performance-optimized mode this week against its usual week. That mode starts and runs faster but burns more DBUs; worth it for time-critical pipelines, but non-urgent jobs can switch to Standard mode and pay for fewer DBUs.
- **How to read it:** Serverless jobs have two performance modes. Both bill at the SAME $/DBU rate; the difference is DBU consumption. Performance-optimized (the default) prioritises fast startup and runs on quicker capacity, so it consumes more DBUs; Standard tolerates a slower cold start and uses fewer DBUs for the same work. The account's list-price spend in Performance-optimized mode this week (complete days) is set against its usual week (the median of the prior four weeks). vs_normal is how many times that normal this week is, and that sets the alert. Each row is one job with its spend, DBUs and share of the account's spend in this mode. usd_7d is the spend exposed to a Standard-mode switch, not a guaranteed saving.
- **What to do:** For latency-tolerant jobs - scheduled batch, backfills, overnight ETL, anything with no downstream deadline - turn OFF ‘Performance optimization’ on the job's serverless compute (Job → task → Compute → Serverless → Performance optimized). That accepts a slower cold start in exchange for fewer DBUs. Keep Performance-optimized only where a run has a real SLA or a waiting consumer. Prove the change with M6 (the job's Performance-optimized line should fall, replaced by a smaller Standard line).
- **Alerts:** Account Performance-optimized serverless job spend this week (a total) versus the account's normal (median of the account's prior four weekly totals (complete days)): high at 3x, medium at 2x, medium when the account has no prior history; rows are the top contributors with their share
- **Prove the fix with:** M6 Serverless job cost by performance mode
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.billing.list_prices`, `system.billing.usage`, `system.lakeflow.job_run_timeline`, `system.lakeflow.jobs`

### Spend Trends & Attribution

Where the money goes and which way it is trending - by product, by job, by pipeline - so a rising cost is spotted early and traced to an owner.

#### H10 Spend by product (SKU)

- **What it finds:** Shows daily spend and usage for every Databricks product, so a rising cost trend is visible early.
- **How to read it:** Scan for products that appear or accelerate week over week. Usage (DBUs) and dollars can rank DIFFERENTLY - a high-usage product on a cheap rate can sit below a lower-usage product on an expensive one, so sort by dollars to see what actually moves the bill. Genuinely free products keep a blank dollar figure but stay visible, so nothing can hide.
- **What to do:** Put budgets behind the trends - account-wide or scoped by workspace/tag, with up to four alert thresholds each. For AI features like Genie, per-user budgets add thresholds and an optional block switch.
- **Alerts:** Informational (no alert)
- **Prove the fix with:** M2 Exact $ per job and per pipeline
- **Reads:** `system.billing.list_prices`, `system.billing.usage`

#### H11 Jobs - weekly movers

- **What it finds:** Shows week-over-week usage per job, so you can tell a genuinely new workload apart from a cost that crept up with no story.
- **How to read it:** Rank the current week, then compare each mover's trajectory. A brand-new job appearing is expected growth - forecast it. An existing job trending up with no new work behind it is the optimisation target. A doubling bill is only a problem if it isn't new work.
- **What to do:** Tag jobs consistently (team / domain / environment) so cost has an owner; turn the daily thresholds you already watch into budget alerts; and review the top movers monthly, applying read-amplification and spill analysis inside them.
- **Alerts:** Informational (no alert)
- **Prove the fix with:** M2 Exact $ per job and per pipeline
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.billing.usage`, `system.lakeflow.job_run_timeline`, `system.lakeflow.jobs`

#### H12 Jobs - who got slower?

- **What it finds:** Shows average run time per job per week, so you can spot a job that is quietly getting slower over time.
- **How to read it:** A job whose duration creeps up while its input grows only linearly is accumulating an inefficiency - growing scans, spill, or the wrong compute size. Cross-check the risers against the weekly movers (are they also the cost movers?) and check their clusters for idle cores.
- **What to do:** For each riser, look one level deeper: cluster utilisation during runs, then the shuffle/spill per stage in the Spark UI. Right-size on evidence. Spot instances suit retryable batch jobs - but not continuous pipelines, where an eviction restarts work and costs more than it saves.
- **Alerts:** Informational (no alert)
- **Prove the fix with:** M2 Exact $ per job and per pipeline
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.billing.usage`, `system.lakeflow.job_run_timeline`, `system.lakeflow.jobs`

#### H13 Pipelines - cost per pipeline

- **What it finds:** Shows weekly usage per Lakeflow (DLT) pipeline, so pipelines of similar size can be compared and the expensive outlier found.
- **How to read it:** Rank by usage; for the expensive ones, divide by table count for a rough cost-per-table comparison. A pipeline that costs far more per table than its peers is usually explained by machine type, on-demand vs spot, or low CPU use (see Idle Cores). Check the pipeline's event log for WARN/ERROR messages too.
- **What to do:** Prefer fewer, larger workers (less shuffle) and a bigger driver when one pipeline drives many tables; start small and step up on evidence; avoid spot for continuous change-data pipelines; and consider splitting a mega-pipeline. For merge-heavy targets: turn on Photon and deletion vectors, keep the real business key as the merge key and put the time column in the ordering/clustering, never in the key.
- **Alerts:** Informational (no alert)
- **Prove the fix with:** M2 Exact $ per job and per pipeline
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.billing.usage`, `system.lakeflow.pipelines`

#### H20 Frequently-failing jobs

- **What it finds:** Checks how much compute the account burned on job runs that failed this week against its usual week; every failed run still pays for setup and partial execution before it dies. Then names the jobs behind it.
- **How to read it:** A job that fails wastes the DBUs of every failed attempt (startup + however far it got). The hours the account spent on runs that ended in failure this week are set against its usual week (the median of the prior four weeks). vs_normal is how many times that normal this week is, and that sets the alert. Each row is one job that failed this week with its run count, failed-run count, failure rate, wasted hours and share of the account's wasted hours. A high failure rate on a frequent job is both a reliability problem and a steady cost leak.
- **What to do:** Root-cause the recurring failures (read the termination reason and the run logs); add an early-abort timeout so a doomed run dies fast instead of burning a full window; and pause or fix broken schedules that never succeed.
- **Alerts:** Account compute on failed job runs this week (a total) versus the account's normal (median of the account's prior four weekly totals): high at 3x, medium at 2x, medium when the account has no prior history; rows are the top contributors with their share
- **Prove the fix with:** M2 Exact $ per job and per pipeline
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.billing.usage`, `system.lakeflow.job_run_timeline`, `system.lakeflow.jobs`

#### H23 Predictive Optimization cost drivers

- **What it finds:** Checks the account's Predictive Optimization DBUs over the last 7 complete days against its usual week, then names the tables whose auto-maintenance (compaction, clustering, vacuum) drove it.
- **How to read it:** Predictive Optimization is the automatic maintenance Databricks runs on your Unity Catalog managed tables: OPTIMIZE/compaction, liquid clustering, VACUUM and stats. You pay per DBU of that work, so the bill tracks how much data is being written and reorganised. The account's PO DBUs in the last 7 complete days are set against its usual week (the median of the four 7-day periods before). vs_normal is how many times that normal this week is, and that sets the alert. Each row is one table + operation type with its PO DBUs this period and the one before, the change, and its share of the account's PO DBUs. dbu_increase is in DBUs, not dollars; multiply by your PO SKU rate for an approximate cost. (The windows are trailing complete 7-day periods, not calendar weeks, so today's partial day never distorts the comparison.)
- **What to do:** Trace the top risers to a cause: (1) more ingestion / writes into that table → expected if it is genuine new data; (2) a table or whole schema newly brought under PO → confirm you actually want PO managing it; (3) a move to liquid clustering or a clustering-key change → a one-off re-cluster that settles after a week or two. Then right-size the SCOPE: enabling PO across prod usually makes sense, but for dev/staging/lower environments decide per use-case - you can leave PO off there and instead run table maintenance (OPTIMIZE/VACUUM) on a schedule, less often. Re-run this check next week; a one-off re-cluster or a narrowed PO scope should pull the top table's dbu_increase back down.
- **Alerts:** Account Predictive Optimization DBUs, last 7 complete days (a total) versus the account's normal (median of the account's four prior 7-day totals): high at 3x, medium at 2x, medium when the account has no prior history; rows are the top contributors with their share
- **Prove the fix with:** n/a
- **Reads:** `system.storage.predictive_optimization_operations_history`

## Tune practices

Practices are grouped by the 4-step maturity model.

### Step 1: Attribute

Know whose cost is whose. Tag every resource and give finance a billing-admin view, so spend can be traced to a team, project, or environment.

#### Tag everything

- **Why it matters:** Tags are labels on your compute - team, project, environment. They are the foundation of everything else: without them, spend is one big anonymous number you can't split, attribute, or budget by.
- **Typical impact:** the foundation
- **How:** ['Three tiers of tag: general (team/owner), contextual (project), operational (environment).', 'Classic compute is tagged in the cluster/job/warehouse definition; serverless is tagged through Budget Policies (which auto-apply on creation).', 'A Budget Policy is different from a Budget - the policy applies tags; the budget alerts on spend.', 'Enforce tags through compute policy so nothing untagged can be created, and run a compliance job to catch gaps.']

#### Give finance the billing-admin role

- **Why it matters:** The billing-admin role gives finance a focused view of spend (the billing system tables and price list) without making them full platform admins. It separates ‘who watches the money’ from ‘who runs the platform’.
- **Typical impact:** separation of duties
- **How:** ['Billing admins can read system.billing.usage and system.billing.list_prices.', "It's a narrower role than account admin - right for a finance/FinOps function.", 'Workspace admins need the billing-admin role added to manage all cost policies.']

### Step 2: Report

See the spend. Use system tables and the built-in dashboards to watch usage, rank the top spenders, and forecast - then set budget alerts.

#### Built-in cost dashboards

- **Why it matters:** Databricks ships ready-made dashboards for account usage, jobs, DBSQL, and model serving. They give you a top-N ranking, efficiency signals, and AI forecasts out of the box - no building required.
- **Typical impact:** zero to build
- **How:** ['Account Usage dashboard: usage overview, top-N spenders, tag analysis, AI forecast.', 'Jobs Monitoring dashboard: top jobs by potential saving, CPU/memory efficiency, outdated runtimes.', 'DBSQL Monitoring dashboard: cost by user and by source (notebook / job / SQL / Genie), query drill-down.', 'Model Serving dashboard: batch, pay-per-token, and provisioned-throughput cost attribution.']

#### Budget alerts

- **Why it matters:** A budget watches spend - account-wide or scoped by workspace or tag - and emails you when it crosses a threshold. It is an alert, not a hard block (except Genie, which can block).
- **Typical impact:** up to 4 thresholds
- **How:** ['Set account-wide or scoped by workspace/tag, with up to four alert thresholds each.', 'Budgets are based on list price, so read them relative to your contract.', 'Genie budgets add per-user thresholds and an optional block switch - the one place spend can actually be stopped, not just alerted.']

### Step 3: Control

Set the guardrails. Compute policies cap runaway sizing, statement timeouts stop runaway queries, and rate limits protect model endpoints.

#### Set a statement timeout

- **Why it matters:** Left alone, a query can run for TWO DAYS before Databricks stops it. A runaway or hung statement can burn compute that whole time. Set a sane workspace timeout (say 2-4 hours) so it stops itself.
- **Typical impact:** default is 2 DAYS
- **How:** ['The platform default STATEMENT_TIMEOUT is 2 days - almost never what you want for ad-hoc.', 'Set it workspace-wide; long ETL can raise its own limit per session.', 'It caps EXECUTION time (time actually running), not queue or fetch time.', 'Changing the workspace value restarts running warehouses - schedule the change.', 'Baseline the offenders and confirm zero afterwards with the ‘Timeout scoreboard’ measure.']

#### Compute policies to stop sprawl

- **Why it matters:** A compute policy sets the rules for what users can create - a maximum DBU-per-hour, how many clusters, which machine types, and which runtime. It stops one person from spinning up something enormous by accident.
- **Typical impact:** cap DBU/hr & clusters
- **How:** ['Cap DBU/hr and max clusters per user to prevent runaway sizing.', 'Allowlist instance types (or use regex) to keep people on sensible machine families.', 'Enforce the latest long-term-support runtime for automatic security and performance updates.', "Enforce tagging through the policy so cost attribution can't be skipped."]

#### Spot VMs for retryable batch

- **Why it matters:** Spot (preemptible) VMs are much cheaper cloud machines that can be reclaimed at short notice. Perfect for batch jobs that can retry - but NOT for continuous pipelines, where a reclaim restarts work and can cost more than it saves.
- **Typical impact:** big infra discount
- **How:** ['Use PREEMPTIBLE_WITH_FALLBACK so the job falls back to on-demand if spot is unavailable.', 'On GCP there is no ‘first N on-demand’ control that some other clouds have - plan for the driver.', 'Great for retryable batch jobs; avoid for continuous change-data pipelines.']

#### Guardrails for AI spend

- **Why it matters:** There is no self-serve, real-time dollar hard-cap for ai_query today. So the controls are: don't hand expensive models to everyone (permissions are the kill-switch), alert on the hourly token rate, and default pipelines to a cheap model.
- **Typical impact:** no real-time $ cap exists
- **How:** ["Governance gate: don't grant EXECUTE on Sonnet/Opus-class models to every pipeline by default - grant per team, on request, with a named owner.", 'Default classification/extraction pipelines to a cheap model (e.g. Haiku); require justification for expensive models at batch scale.', 'A rate limit of 0 will NOT cap a runaway batch job - use the token-rate alert (the ‘Runaway AI spend’ hunt) as the fast substitute.', 'For sustained workloads, Provisioned Throughput converts pay-per-token into a fixed, bounded cost.']

### Step 4: Optimise

Tune the platform. Pick the right compute for each workload, and use features like serverless, Liquid Clustering, and compression to do the same work for less.

#### Use Job compute for jobs, not All-Purpose

- **Why it matters:** All-Purpose clusters are for interactive notebook work. Running a scheduled job on one costs 2-3× more than running it on Job compute, which spins up for the job and shuts down after.
- **Typical impact:** 2-3× cheaper
- **How:** ['All-Purpose is billed at a higher DBU rate than Jobs compute for the same machine.', 'A job pinned to an always-on All-Purpose cluster also pays for all the idle time between runs.', 'Move scheduled work to Jobs Compute (ephemeral) or Serverless Jobs.', 'Spot the offenders with the ‘Idle Compute’ hunts, then confirm the DBU moves to the jobs product after you switch.']

#### Serverless for spiky and ad-hoc work

- **Why it matters:** Serverless starts in under ten seconds and charges only while it is working - there is no idle cost. For bursty, unpredictable, or small workloads it is typically 25%+ cheaper than keeping a classic warehouse warm.
- **Typical impact:** 25%+ savings
- **How:** ["Sub-10-second startup means you don't keep compute running ‘just in case’.", 'Zero idle cost - it stops the moment work finishes.', 'Best fit: ad-hoc SQL, bursty BI, small or unpredictable jobs.', 'Steady, predictable, high-utilisation load can still be cheaper on right-sized classic with committed-use discounts - decide by load shape (see the ‘Queue Pressure’ hunt).']

#### Photon: know the break-even

- **Why it matters:** Photon is a faster engine, but it bills more DBUs per hour - about 2.9× on job clusters and ~2× on All-Purpose. It only saves money if it cuts the runtime by MORE than that multiplier. Great for heavy SQL scans; wasteful on light work.
- **Typical impact:** 2.9× DBU on jobs
- **How:** ['Photon speeds up scans, joins, and aggregations - CPU-heavy SQL work.', 'It does NOT speed up UDFs, streaming state, or lightweight jobs - those pay the premium with no runtime win.', 'Rule of thumb: enable it where it cuts runtime by more than the DBU multiplier; measure before/after with the ‘Prove’ page.', 'On classic Jobs there is no Photon price premium (it bills the same) - there, Photon is a pure speed win.']

#### Liquid Clustering, Predictive Optimization & Zstd

- **Why it matters:** Three low-effort storage wins: Liquid Clustering keeps data laid out so queries skip what they don't need; Predictive Optimization runs the housekeeping for you; and Zstd compression can cut storage up to 40% with no maintenance.
- **Typical impact:** up to 40% storage
- **How:** ['Liquid Clustering: turn on for all new Delta tables - ~2.5× faster than the old Z-order approach, up to a 150 TB table size (as of Aug 2026).', 'Predictive Optimization: hands-off compaction and clustering - but only on Unity Catalog MANAGED tables, and worth DISABLING on non-prod schemas that never get scanned.', 'Zstd compression: up to 40% storage savings on UC managed tables, zero maintenance.']

### M1 Per-query attributed cost (shared warehouses)

- **What it shows:** Prices individual queries on a shared warehouse by allocating each a slice of the warehouse-hour's cost in proportion to its execution time.
- **How to read it:** Warehouses bill by uptime, not by query, so per-query cost is an ALLOCATION. Each query gets a share of its warehouse-hour's cost against the warehouse's cluster-hours of capacity - so the shares never exceed the real cost. Use it to price a specific expensive query (e.g. an investigation scan) and to show a per-query before/after.
- **Use when:** you want to price one specific expensive query (e.g. an investigation scan) and show a per-query before/after.
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.billing.list_prices`, `system.billing.usage`, `system.compute.warehouses`, `system.query.history`

### M3 The “After” Scoreboard - weekly $ per warehouse

- **What it shows:** The headline proof. Weekly spend per warehouse over eight weeks - fix something, then watch this line step down.
- **How to read it:** Every warehouse-level fix (chatter, auto-stop, placement, downsizing) lands here. Compare weeks of the same-shape workload. Remember classic warehouses carry a separate cloud-infrastructure bill next to this usage line.
- **Use when:** you changed a warehouse's size, auto-stop, or moved a workload - the headline proof. Watch its weekly line step down.
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.billing.list_prices`, `system.billing.usage`, `system.compute.warehouses`

### M1b Warehouse idle cost - the residual

- **What it shows:** For each warehouse: total cost, the part that did actual work, and the unallocated (idle) remainder - plus average utilisation.
- **How to read it:** High idle cost with low utilisation points to an auto-stop or right-sizing conversation, not a query problem. This is an ALLOCATION PROXY, not measured idle: it uses each warehouse's cluster-hours of capacity as the denominator, a sound but simplified floor. The dollars are Databricks list-price DBU cost - Finance applies your contract rate and (for classic) the separate cloud bill.
- **Use when:** you suspect a warehouse is billing while idle - shows the idle $ and utilisation so you can prove an auto-stop / right-sizing fix.
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.billing.list_prices`, `system.billing.usage`, `system.compute.warehouses`, `system.query.history`

### M2 Exact $ per job and per pipeline

- **What it shows:** Exact weekly dollars for each job and pipeline - the before/after for any job or pipeline fix is two rows of this table.
- **How to read it:** Because usage carries the entity ID, this is DIRECTLY attributed (not an allocation) wherever the ID is populated. Usage with no job/pipeline ID (some shared or serverless products) shows up as its own ‘unattributed’ row rather than being dropped, so the dollars reconcile.
- **Use when:** you fixed a job or pipeline (moved to Jobs compute, right-sized, cut failures) - the before/after is two rows of this table.
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.billing.list_prices`, `system.billing.usage`, `system.lakeflow.job_run_timeline`, `system.lakeflow.jobs`, `system.lakeflow.pipelines`

### M6 Serverless job cost by performance mode

- **What it shows:** For the top serverless jobs: weekly dollars split into Performance-optimized vs Standard mode - the direct before/after for a performance-mode change.
- **How to read it:** Both modes bill at the same $/DBU rate, so this splits each job's weekly serverless spend by performance_target to make a mode change visible. Before a change, a job's spend sits entirely in the Performance-optimized column; after you disable performance optimization, subsequent weeks shift into the Standard column at a lower DBU total for the same work. Limited to the top jobs by Performance-optimized spend so the table stays legible. List-price DBU cost, directly attributed by job_id.
- **Use when:** you switched a serverless job from Performance-optimized to Standard mode (H22) - watch that job's Performance-optimized column fall, replaced by a smaller Standard column.
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.billing.list_prices`, `system.billing.usage`, `system.lakeflow.job_run_timeline`, `system.lakeflow.jobs`

### M5 Timeout guardrail scoreboard

- **What it shows:** Counts statements per warehouse that ran longer than a one-hour cap - your baseline before setting timeouts, and zero after.
- **How to read it:** A statement timeout caps EXECUTION time (time actually running), not total elapsed time (which includes queueing and fetching results), so this tests execution time - what a timeout would really have killed. Baseline now (expect the multi-hour offenders); confirm zero after the caps go in.
- **Use when:** you set (or plan to set) a statement timeout - baseline the over-cap runners now, confirm zero after.
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.compute.warehouses`, `system.query.history`

### M4 Benchmark a fix - per-statement efficiency, week over week

- **What it shows:** For the busiest warehouses, average read / spill / duration per statement each week - so after a fix you can see the per-run numbers fall.
- **How to read it:** This is the before/after benchmark. Pick the warehouse behind a fix and read down its weekly rows: average bytes read, spilled, and execution minutes per statement. A successful read-amplification or spill fix shows the averages step DOWN in the weeks after you ship it, while the row count (runs) holds - proof the work got cheaper per run, not just quieter.
- **Use when:** you fixed read-amplification or spill and want to prove work got cheaper PER RUN (not just quieter) - watch the averages fall while run count holds.
- **Reads:** `system.access.workspaces_latest`, `system.compute.warehouses`, `system.query.history`
